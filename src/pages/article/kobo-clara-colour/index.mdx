The <a href="https://us.kobobooks.com/products/kobo-clara-colour" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Kobo Clara Colour</a> changed that. ## The problem with reading

Reading a physical book always wore me out faster than I thought it should. I would start a chapter and find my attention sliding off the page. I assumed it was me — short attention span, not a book person.

What I did not know was that part of the friction was the font itself. Standard typefaces are designed for sighted readers without processing differences. If your brain works differently — if letters tend to rotate or swap on you — those same fonts quietly make reading harder every single line.

---

## OpenDyslexic changes the equation

The Kobo Clara Colour ships with <a href="https://opendyslexic.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">OpenDyslexic</a> as a built-in font option. OpenDyslexic was designed specifically to reduce the visual confusion that can come with dyslexia. Each letter has a heavier bottom, which is meant to keep the brain from flipping or rotating it. The shapes are more distinct from one another.

Switching to it on the Kobo was immediate. The text just sat there on the page, stable, not asking anything of me before I could read it. That sounds small. It is not small.

## What the research says

I looked into this, and the research is not what I expected. The most reliable summary is a 2026 <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-026-00389-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">meta-analysis in Annals of Dyslexia</a> by Azzarello and colleagues. It pooled 15 studies of OpenDyslexic, Dyslexie and similar fonts, 688 dyslexic readers in all. The overall effect on reading speed and accuracy was negligible (g = -0.04, 95% CI -0.15 to 0.07). The authors conclude that these fonts have no consistent or reliable effect, although some typographic features may help legibility.

The individual studies point the same way.

* <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5629233" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Wery and Diliberto</a> compared OpenDyslexic with Arial and Times New Roman in 12 children aged 9 to 12. They found no improvement in reading rate or accuracy, for individuals or for the group. It is a small study, but a careful one.
* <a href="https://doi.org/10.1145/2513383.2513447" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Rello and Baeza-Yates</a> tracked the eyes of dyslexic readers across twelve fonts. Sans serif, monospaced and roman styles beat serif, proportional and italic ones, and OpenDyslexic was not easier to read than common fonts.
* <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-017-0154-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Kuster and colleagues</a> tested Dyslexie, a similar font, on 170 children with dyslexia. They found no gain in speed or accuracy over Arial, and the children preferred Arial and Times New Roman.

The one study that found a benefit is useful, because of why. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.1527" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Marinus and colleagues</a> found that 39 low-progress readers read Dyslexie about 7 percent faster than Arial. When Arial was given the same spacing, the difference disappeared. The authors concluded that the benefit came from the spacing, not the letter shapes.

That points to spacing. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1205566109" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Zorzi and colleagues</a> tested 74 dyslexic children and found that extra letter spacing improved reading accuracy and speed. <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Galliussi and colleagues</a> added a catch: wider letter spacing slowed reading unless the word spacing grew with it. Their dyslexia-friendly letterforms made no measurable difference. One more paper, <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci8050089" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Bachmann and Mengheri</a>, reported a benefit for the EasyReading font. Its authors are connected to the font's developer, and I did not read the full paper, so I would not lean on it.

Newer work, since 2021, fits the same picture.

* <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ridd.2021.104065" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Stagg and colleagues</a> tested 32 dyslexic adolescents. Extra-large letter spacing improved reading speed, more so than for the comparison group, and cut missed-word errors. Coloured overlays did nothing measurable.
* <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.1727" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Joseph and colleagues</a> (2022) compared Dyslexie with Calibri in 71 children, with spacing held constant. Word and passage reading did not differ. Naming single letters was faster in Dyslexie, which suggests distinct letter shapes may help with letter identification and little else.
* <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s40617-025-01048-x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Machado and colleagues</a> (2025) tested Dyslexie in adults. Accuracy and efficiency did not differ between fonts, but participants varied in which one they preferred.

The Kobo adjusts line spacing and margins, which is different from the letter and word spacing in those studies. I do not know of a Kobo setting that changes letter spacing.

None of that changes my experience. OpenDyslexic works for me, and a study describes a group average, not one reader. It does change what I would tell someone else. Try the fonts, try the spacing, and keep whatever works. Do not expect a font to replace reading instruction. Marinus herself has argued that intensive, phonics-based teaching is the proven help. That is the real argument for a device that lets you change all of it.

## Font flexibility beyond one choice

What makes the Kobo's font system genuinely useful is that it goes further than a single accessible option. You can adjust:

- **Font family** — OpenDyslexic, Georgia, Gill Sans, Kobo Nickel, and more
- **Font weight** — thin to bold, independently of the typeface
- **Font size** — wide range, adjustable on the fly
- **Line spacing and margins** — control the density of text on the page

This matters because accessibility needs are not uniform. What helps one person read better may not help another. Having real control over the reading environment means you can tune it to your specific situation rather than accepting defaults.

I keep OpenDyslexic as my default, bump up the font size a notch above what I would have picked a year ago, and widen the line spacing slightly. That combination works for me. It took maybe five minutes to find it.

## What has actually changed

I have read more in the months since getting the Clara Colour than in the several years before it combined. That is not an exaggeration. I have finished books I had been "meaning to read" for years. I pick up the Kobo most evenings now the way I used to mindlessly pick up my phone.

The device is compact — roughly the size of a paperback — and light enough to hold one-handed. The e-ink screen means no blue light fighting my sleep the way a phone screen would. The battery lasts weeks. None of those things are the reason I use it, but they all make it easier to keep using it.

A physical book does not give you any of this. The font is fixed, the size is fixed, the contrast is whatever the publisher chose. For most people that is fine. For me, those fixed constraints were quietly making reading harder than it needed to be. The Kobo removes them entirely — same words, same stories, but on your terms.

If you are comparing this to a Kindle, the reading experience is similar in feel, but the Kindle ties you more tightly to Amazon — your books live in their ecosystem and moving them elsewhere is not straightforward. Kobo supports open ebook formats, so your library is more portable and less dependent on a single storefront.

The reason I use it is that reading does not feel like work anymore.

## Worth considering if you have struggled with reading

If you have written yourself off as a non-reader, or found that you start books but never finish them, I would not assume the problem is you before you have tried adjusting the reading environment. Font choice specifically is underrated. Most reading apps on phones and tablets treat font as a cosmetic preference. The Kobo treats it as something that affects whether reading actually works.
