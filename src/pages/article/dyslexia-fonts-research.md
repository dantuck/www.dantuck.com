I use OpenDyslexic, and it works for me. I wrote about <a href="/article/kobo-clara-colour">how the Kobo Clara Colour got me reading again</a>, and the font was a big part of that.

Then I looked at the research. It does not say what I expected. This article is the longer version of what I found.

## The short answer

Dyslexia-specific fonts do not reliably make people read faster or more accurately. Spacing does, in some studies. That is the pattern across the research I could find.

## The meta-analysis

The most reliable summary is a 2026 <a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-026-00389-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">meta-analysis in Annals of Dyslexia</a> by Azzarello and colleagues. It pooled 15 studies of fonts such as OpenDyslexic and Dyslexie, with 688 dyslexic readers in all. Each study compared a dyslexia font with a standard font like Arial or Times New Roman.

The overall effect on reading speed and accuracy was negligible. The authors report g = -0.04, with a 95% confidence interval of -0.15 to 0.07. That interval includes zero, so the data do not show a benefit. The authors conclude the fonts have no consistent or reliable effect, although some typographic features may help legibility.

A meta-analysis is only as good as the studies in it. So here are some of those studies.

---

## Studies on specific fonts

<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5629233" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Wery and Diliberto</a> tested OpenDyslexic against Arial and Times New Roman in 12 children aged 9 to 12. They found no improvement in reading rate or accuracy, for individual children or for the group. It is a small study. The authors concluded there may be no benefit to converting print materials to this font.

<a href="https://doi.org/10.1145/2513383.2513447" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Rello and Baeza-Yates</a> tracked the eyes of dyslexic readers across twelve fonts. Sans serif, monospaced and roman styles beat serif, proportional and italic ones. OpenDyslexic was not easier to read than the common fonts.

<a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-017-0154-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Kuster and colleagues</a> tested Dyslexie, a similar font, on 170 children with dyslexia. They found no gain in speed or accuracy over Arial. In a second experiment the children preferred Arial and Times New Roman.

<a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.1727" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Joseph and colleagues</a> compared Dyslexie with Calibri in 71 children, with spacing held constant. Word and passage reading did not differ. Naming single letters was faster in Dyslexie. That suggests distinct letter shapes may help with letter identification, and little beyond that.

<a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s40617-025-01048-x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Machado and colleagues</a> tested adults. Accuracy and efficiency did not differ between fonts, but participants varied in which one they liked. The study is described as preliminary.

## The one study that found a benefit

<a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.1527" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Marinus and colleagues</a> found that 39 low-progress readers read Dyslexie about 7 percent faster than Arial. That sounds like a win for the font.

Then the researchers gave Arial the same spacing as Dyslexie. The difference disappeared. They concluded the benefit came from the spacing, not the letter shapes.

---

## What spacing does

That result points at the actual mechanism. Researchers think crowding is part of the problem for some dyslexic readers. Neighbouring letters interfere with each other, and more space reduces the interference.

<a href="https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1205566109" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Zorzi and colleagues</a> tested 74 dyslexic children and found that extra letter spacing improved reading accuracy and speed.

<a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ridd.2021.104065" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Stagg and colleagues</a> tested 32 dyslexic adolescents. Extra-large letter spacing improved reading speed, more so than for the comparison group, and it cut missed-word errors. Coloured overlays made no measurable difference.

<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Galliussi and colleagues</a> added a catch. Wider letter spacing slowed reading unless the space between words grew too. Their dyslexia-friendly letterforms made no measurable difference.

So the useful rule is this: open up the letters, and open up the words with them.

## A study I would not lean on

<a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci8050089" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Bachmann and Mengheri</a> reported a benefit for a font called EasyReading. Its authors are connected to the font's developer, and the paper does not separate the effect of the letter shapes from the effect of the spacing. I did not read the full paper, so I would not build on it.

---

## What I would try

I am not a clinician, and none of this replaces reading instruction. Marinus herself has argued that intensive, phonics-based teaching is the help with proven results. A font is a comfort setting, not an intervention.

With that said, these are cheap to test:

* Raise letter spacing, and word spacing with it.
* Try a plain sans serif font such as Arial before a specialist font.
* Change one setting at a time, so you can tell what helped.
* Keep whatever feels easier, even if a study would predict no difference. A study reports a group average. You are one reader.

That last point is why I still use OpenDyslexic. The research gave me better questions to ask, not a reason to stop.
